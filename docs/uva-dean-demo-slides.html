As AI becomes part of this process, preserving the connections between these activities becomes increasingly important.

In my own workflow, I have tried to address this through a shared memory vault that sits above individual project folders, preserving history, decisions and useful context across projects. A central memory agent draws on this information to coordinate work, send requests to project-specific conversations and oversee the resulting changes. Yet much of the coordination still requires manual effort: bringing material from webpages, articles, code and papers into the agent through screenshots, computer interaction or copied text, then transferring its findings and changes back into the relevant tools. This arrangement helps maintain continuity, but moving information between tools and keeping the broader research context coherent remain challenges.

The same challenge becomes more pronounced in research teams and labs, where knowledge must outlast any individual’s involvement. Research assistants and PhD students join, move between projects and eventually leave. New members need to understand not only existing methods and tools, but also the reasoning behind them. A successor may inherit files without knowing why particular decisions were made, which approaches failed or which questions remain unresolved. Differences in technical experience can further complicate both knowledge transfer and the adoption of AI tools.

This raises a question beyond which model or tool to use: how can AI support research while preserving the context that gives it direction? General-purpose tools such as Claude Code and Codex offer powerful capabilities, but applying them across a research project still requires people to assemble context, connect tools and maintain continuity. Researchers and disciplines also differ in their methods, preferences and ways of working. A useful research environment should accommodate these differences while supporting—not displacing—the intuition, creativity and inspiration that guide exploration.

Our experience developing and evaluating agentic solutions within AISO and through work associated with ABN AMRO has highlighted the importance of explainability, traceability and transparency. Producing an answer is only part of the task. Researchers also need to inspect the supporting evidence, follow the history of an experiment and understand the limitations of a result. Reproducibility, the ability to work locally, privacy and control over external connections are equally important considerations.

Building on these experiences, AISO Research proposes exploring this direction through ros.ai, a visual research sandbox for developing ideas with AI. The aim is to connect research materials, conversations, decisions and experiment histories in an environment where researchers can build on their work without repeatedly reconstructing its context. Tool coordination and routine tasks could happen behind the scenes, while researchers retain control over their questions, methods and the structure of their exploration. The sandbox would also provide a practical setting for investigating how these capabilities can support research and learning.

We propose beginning with the community. Workshops with students and research assistants, alongside interviews with early-career researchers, would help us understand how people explore ideas, where they lose context and what prevents them from using AI effectively. These activities would combine practical experimentation with opportunities to learn sound research practices. Findings from the workshops and initial use cases would guide the platform’s development and help determine how to extend it to professors and broader research teams.

For the University of Amsterdam, this initiative could offer a practical way to support the dean’s vision and the university’s AI research programme. Subject to confirming the university’s plans, the sandbox could provide space for rapid prototyping and learning while an official AI environment takes shape. Lessons from these experiments could inform future decisions about tools, researcher support and research practices, connecting student-led exploration with the university’s broader ambitions.

To make the proposal concrete, we are considering two demonstration cases: a finance or FinTech use case, potentially drawing on the ABN AMRO work, and a case accessible to nontechnical users, potentially based on statistical work within AISO. Both remain provisional, with their scope to be agreed on Monday. Together, they would illustrate how preserved context, a visible research history and understandable experimentation can help students and researchers move from an initial question towards meaningful learning and useful research results.

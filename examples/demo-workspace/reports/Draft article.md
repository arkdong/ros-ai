# AI adoption differs by business size. Banks need to ask what sits behind the label.

*Unpublished research draft prepared for the ros.ai demo from the CBS data and the Finding notes on this canvas. The figures come from the Code block's saved Docker run of 23 September 2026. This is not an ABN AMRO publication.*

For a business considering artificial intelligence, a national adoption percentage is a starting point for a conversation. It is a weak substitute for understanding the job the technology will do. A useful banking discussion should connect a proposed tool to a specific process, a cost and a way to check whether it works. The size of the business can help frame that discussion, but it should not decide the answer.

## A clear difference, within a defined population

In the CBS 2024 figures, reported AI use rises from 17.8% for businesses with 10–19 persons employed to 59.2% for those with 500 or more. Our calculation puts the gap at 41.4 percentage points. These provisional estimates cover NACE sectors C–N and Q. Businesses with fewer than ten persons employed are excluded. The chart therefore cannot describe all Dutch small businesses. [CBS, tables 2.1.3 and 2.2.1](https://www.cbs.nl/en-gb/longread/aanvullende-statistische-diensten/2025/ai-monitor-2024/2-use-of-ai-technology-by-dutch-companies)

*Figure 1 is in the Code block: enterprises using at least one of seven AI technologies, by persons employed, 2024, with the CBS published 95% intervals.*

## The challenge changes the interpretation

Our first interpretation asked whether smaller Dutch businesses were falling behind in AI value creation. The evidence does not support that wording. Among the 500+ group, AI users account for 79.5% of turnover but 59.2% of enterprises. The 20.3-point difference compares denominators; it does not measure sales produced by AI. Nor do these aggregates establish productivity, profit or causality. [CBS, chapter 2](https://www.cbs.nl/en-gb/longread/aanvullende-statistische-diensten/2025/ai-monitor-2024/2-use-of-ai-technology-by-dutch-companies)

*Figure 2 is in the Code block: shares of enterprises using AI and of turnover belonging to AI users. Revenue may arise from any activity of those firms.*

## Implementation deserves its own conversation

Recent banking commentary points to a practical reason for asking what sits behind adoption. ABN AMRO's June 2026 Insights article, based on research with MWM2 among 777 organisations, discusses digital risks and gaps in smaller firms' preparedness. This is a separate survey and context, not a continuation of our CBS series. It gives us a reason to ask about controls alongside potential benefits. [ABN AMRO Insights, 23 June 2026](https://www.abnamro.nl/nl/zakelijk/insights/sectoren-en-trends/alle-sectoren/mkb-niet-klaar-voor-nieuwe-digitale-risicos.html)

For a bank such as ABN AMRO, one possible implication is to make an AI discussion more concrete. Ask the business to choose one process: preparing a document, handling a customer query or checking an invoice. What happens today? What would change? Who checks an incorrect answer? These questions are proposed lines of enquiry, not measured findings about bank customers or recommendations to buy a particular product.

## Assess a plan against its own starting point

A proposed investment could set out software fees, integration work, staff training and continuing review. A small trial might record the time taken and the number of corrections before and after introducing the tool. The design would need to account for changes in the work itself. Faster completion would be useful evidence for that process; it would not automatically establish a business-wide productivity improvement.

A lender could then ask how the business expects an operational change to affect cash flow. Does saved time release capacity that can actually be used? Is demand sufficient to turn extra capacity into sales? Are savings large enough to cover maintenance and supervision? Those are hypotheses to investigate with the applicant. A yes-or-no adoption indicator cannot answer them, and this demonstration does not assess any borrower or credit decision.

## Keep exposure and benefit separate

A second possible implication concerns dependence on external services. An implementation discussion could document what data leave the organisation, who has access, how errors are detected and what happens if the supplier changes its service. These are questions for a specific system and its users. Firm size alone cannot show whether arrangements are appropriate, and the adoption chart contains no observation of these controls.

Research could also distinguish a business's own use from the use of its suppliers or customers. A company without an internal AI project may still face changes in purchasing, competition or service delivery. That possibility is a research question raised by the topic, not an effect estimated here. Establishing it would require different data and a declared comparison, rather than stretching the current six size groups into an answer.

## Start with a question that can be checked

The next useful step is a small, well-defined evidence request. A business could describe one proposed use, its expected costs, its review process and the observations that would lead it to continue or stop. A bank could use that account to structure a conversation while evaluating the wider business on its own evidence. The national comparison helps identify questions; the quality of a specific plan depends on the answers.
